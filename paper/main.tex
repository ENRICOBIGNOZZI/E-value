\begin{abstract}
An investment signal should be evaluated through the portfolio it changes,
including estimation error and the cost of reaching that portfolio. We study
sequential comparisons between implementable policies under quadratic utility,
proportional turnover costs and quadratic market impact. A Catoni
supermartingale validates a single adaptive comparison with a certified
conditional second-moment bound. Under a constant positive conditional mean,
past-only blockwise betting incurs logarithmic expected regret in a quadratic
growth certificate and admits a finite-sample power bound. We distinguish this
certificate-optimal bet from log-growth and horizon-power optimization, and
execute a separate predictable Bellman betting experiment. Convex analysis
characterizes the population value of adding a signal, while a uniform
perturbation argument connects that value to estimated policies with separate
trading states. A costless scalar example yields a sharp confidence exponent
and a conditional waiting-benefit coefficient for a vanishing estimated-policy
gain, alongside very low executed finite-horizon power.
A single simulated market combines persistent, weak, decaying
and reviving predictors with structural noise and correlated proxies. The
protocol explicitly separates individual-test validity, economic decision
quality and portfolio performance. Its uniform risk certificate and annual
decision bottleneck can make validation economically slow. We report executed
diagnostics without presuming superiority, global error control or a theorem
of adaptation to changing regimes. A declared extension combines observable
policy geometry with bias-corrected tail betting under eighth return moments;
fixed-reference and full-fund experiments distinguish feasible certification
from its economic waiting costs.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sequential inference; investment signals; e-processes;
transaction costs; portfolio estimation; simulation.

\section{Introduction}
A portfolio manager faces three related decisions: whether to add a signal,
park an incumbent signal, or replace one signal with another. The economically
relevant object is the change in a feasible investment policy. A predictor's
own coefficient, its standalone performance and its contribution to a
portfolio are different quantities. A signal without a direct predictive
coefficient may hedge an incumbent exposure or proxy another characteristic.
Conversely, an authentic predictor may worsen a portfolio when estimated
weights and trading costs are taken into account.

We formulate these decisions as comparisons of two policies constructed before
the returns used to evaluate them. Each policy pays its own trading costs and
keeps its own allocation state. Their observable utility difference feeds a
heavy-tail-robust e-process. An annual search proposes additions, removals and
a restricted set of swaps; the test evaluates those proposals. This separation
allows one to ask both whether the statistical comparison is valid and whether
waiting for its evidence is worthwhile.
We call the annual add--remove--swap implementation PARK/REVIVE; its name
describes available actions, not a guarantee of adaptation to a new regime.

The core analysis has four parts. First, a conditional supermartingale argument
establishes validity for an individual adaptive episode. Second, a stationary
alternative yields an oracle lower bound on power and a logarithmic cost for
estimating the bet. Third, a residual marginal utility and a Schur complement
bound the population benefit of entry under trading costs. Fourth, objective
perturbations control the gap between population values and estimated-policy
comparisons, including the effect of different previous allocations.
We then distinguish optimizing a power certificate, expected log growth and
actual working-model rejection probability by a fixed review horizon.
The last criterion gives a predictable Bellman rule tested separately on fresh
controlled score paths; it does not silently replace the financial methods.

The statistical ingredients build on Catoni-style confidence sequences
\cite{wr} and the broader theory of safe anytime-valid inference \cite{savi}.
Sequential comparison of forecast scores is developed by \cite{henzi} and
\cite{choe}; the latter also treats a weak null about average conditional
score differences. Our T1 uses the datewise conditional-mean null stated
below. It does not establish validity for that broader weak null. All sleeve
returns are observed, so the present setting permits full-information
counterfactual score calculation rather than requiring off-policy importance
weights. We specialize these comparisons to estimated constrained portfolios,
separate trading states and explicit moment certificates.
Sequential scoring-rule selection with exponentiated score products is also
studied in \cite{ssre}, under its stated conditional-factor and moment
assumptions. Sequential selection between estimated forecasting methods is
therefore an existing research problem.
The economic optimization uses the convex trading perspective of
\cite{boyd}. The present contribution is the explicit connection of these
components to comparisons of estimated investment policies with separately
accounted trading states. We do not claim a new general e-process construction
or a new general Schur-complement identity.

Portfolio evaluation of characteristics with risk and trading costs already
appears in \cite{demiguel}; its stock-level trading-netting mechanism is outside
our sleeve-turnover cost model. \cite{carrasco} develops GMM inference for
transaction-cost effects under recursive utility. E-process applications in
finance include risk-forecast backtesting \cite{eback} and comparative
risk-measure backtests \cite{comparativeback}. These precedents preclude a
claim that either economic signal evaluation or sequential financial comparison
is new. The contribution studied here is their specialization to observable
policy-pair scores with separate allocation states, explicit public moment
certificates and an assessment of the cost of waiting. General instance log
optimality and rejection-time theory for portfolio-based e-processes are
developed in \cite{instance}; the portfolios there allocate statistical betting
capital. Our investible portfolios generate utility scores, and T2 below is
only a bound on a quadratic growth certificate, not an instance optimality
result.

Estimation noise in comparisons of nested forecasting models is already
analyzed by \cite{clarkmccracken,clarkwest}. In particular, fitting redundant
parameters can worsen an estimated forecast even when the population models
coincide. We retain such estimation effects when they change traded utility:
our inferential target is an implemented policy comparison, rather than an
estimation-noise-adjusted test of population predictive content.

Multiple testing remains a separate issue. The financial significance of
searching among many predictors is emphasized by \cite{hlz}. Sequential model
confidence sets already provide simultaneous time-uniform coverage for
superior forecasters \cite{smcs}. Our specified
algorithm deliberately applies no correction across candidates or episodes;
its individual 5\% guarantee is therefore not a 5\% guarantee for the entire
search. Nor does an episode rejection certify that a previously profitable
signal remains profitable in the current regime.

An explicit counterexample below makes this distinction operational: an
annual decision can pass both the evidence threshold and the positive-history
filter while the current gain is negative.

\section{The economic object}
Let $A_{t+1}\in\mathbb R^p$ be next-period gross returns of observable signal
sleeves. Investor information $\G_t$ contains observed characteristics and
returns, published risk caps, policy states and past algorithmic decisions,
but no supplied hidden market state or future shock. Allocations $w_t$,
previous targets $b_t$ and bets are $\G_t$-measurable. In the mathematical
results, $\F_t\supseteq\G_t$ denotes the specified pre-return conditioning
filtration; it may equal $\G_t$. The simulation evaluator uses an enlarged
filtration also containing current latent coefficients and the current regime,
but no future innovations. Its state-conditioned oracle moments need not equal
the learner's conditional moments given $\G_t$. Define
\begin{align}
 C_t(w,b)&=c\norm{w-b}_1+\frac\eta2(w-b)'\Lambda_t(w-b),\\
 U_{t+1}(w;b)&=w'A_{t+1}-\frac\gamma2(w'A_{t+1})^2-C_t(w,b),\label{eq:utility}\\
 J_t(w;b)&=w'\mu_t-\frac\gamma2w'M_tw-C_t(w,b),\label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
where $\mu_t=\E[A_{t+1}\mid\F_t]$ and
$M_t=\E[A_{t+1}A_{t+1}'\mid\F_t]$. We require $\gamma>0$, $c,\eta\ge0$
and $\Lambda_t\succeq0$. Whenever the expectations exist,
\begin{equation}
 \E[U_{t+1}(w;b)\mid\F_t]=J_t(w;b).
\end{equation}
This follows by conditional linearity and the raw-second-moment identity.
The conditional null and moment assumptions below must hold for the same
chosen filtration. The analytic public envelopes hold also for the enlarged
simulation filtration and therefore, by the tower property, for the investor
filtration. Economic event labels use the former; they do not establish the
truth or falsity of the investor-filtration episode null.

The raw second moment is essential: $M_t=\Sigma_t+\mu_t\mu_t'$.
Replacing $M_t$ by covariance removes the term
$\gamma(w'\mu_t)^2/2$ and changes the objective. Trading costs are subtracted
after applying the quadratic score to gross returns. Squaring net returns
would also define another objective. Quadratic utility is a scoring criterion;
it is not globally increasing over unbounded returns.

For a support $S\subseteq[p]$, let
\begin{equation}
 \W(S)=\{w:\supp(w)\subseteq S,\ \norm w_1\le L\},\qquad
 V_t(S;b)=\max_{w\in\W(S)}J_t(w;b).
\end{equation}
The empty support permits only $w=0$. Thus $V_t(\varnothing;0)=0$, whereas
$V_t(\varnothing;b)=-C_t(0,b)$: liquidation is not free. For $S\subseteq B$,
feasible-set inclusion gives $V_t(B;b)\ge V_t(S;b)$ at the same state.
This does not imply that an estimated policy with additional signals improves
realized or conditional utility.

The implementation uses $b_t=w_{t-1}$, previous target allocations across
sleeves. This stylized allocator-turnover model does not reconstruct trades
inside each sleeve. Its net return for wealth and Sharpe calculations is
$r^{net}_{t+1}=w_t'A_{t+1}-C_t(w_t,b_t)$; its utility remains
\eqref{eq:utility}. Daily returns are decimals, $c$ is utility per unit of
gross allocation change, $\eta\Lambda_t$ is quadratic cost curvature and
$\gamma$ is quadratic return curvature.

\section{Comparison episodes and the information protocol}
An episode is identified by its start $\tau$, reference support $S$, candidate
support $B$, learning rule, cost parameters and state convention. A forward
move sets $B=S\cup\{j\}$; a removal sets $B=S\setminus\{j\}$; a swap sets
$B=(S\setminus\{i\})\cup\{j\}$. All use the same orientation,
\begin{equation}
 Y_{t+1}=U_{t+1}(B)-U_{t+1}(S).\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
The removal candidate is the policy without the incumbent signal.

At episode inception, the two shadow policies start from the fund's same
actual allocation. Thereafter, each shadow keeps its own previous target and
pays its own turnover and impact. Selecting a candidate changes the fund's
support; its next target is recomputed from the fund's actual previous
allocation. The shadow's accumulated positions are not transferred to the fund.

Estimation uses expanding observations and is refreshed annually. Evidence
uses only utility scores realized after the episode starts. A routine refit of
a predeclared adaptive policy does not rewrite its old scores. Changing the
reference support closes its comparisons and opens new episodes, initialized
at one, on future observations. Expanding estimation and accumulating test
evidence are distinct operations.

\section{Individual sequential validity}
We test the episode null
\begin{equation}
 H_0:\quad\E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\le0\quad\text{at every episode date}.\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
This is stronger than a statement about the unconditional historical average.
Let $v_t^2>0$ be predictable with
$\E[Y_t^2\mid\F_{t-1}]\le v_t^2$. Define the odd Catoni function
\begin{equation}
 \phi(x)=\operatorname{sign}(x)\log(1+|x|+x^2/2).
\end{equation}
It satisfies, for all real $x$,
\begin{equation}
 e^{\phi(x)}\le1+x+x^2/2,\quad
 e^{-\phi(x)}\le1-x+x^2/2,\quad
 \phi(x)\ge x-x^2/2.\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
Indeed $(1+x+x^2/2)(1-x+x^2/2)=1+x^4/4\ge1$; the first two inequalities
follow by considering the sign of $x$. The last follows from
$\phi(x)\ge-\log(1-x+x^2/2)\ge x-x^2/2$.

For predictable $\lambda_t\ge0$, set
\begin{equation}
 L_{\tau,t}=\sum_{s=\tau+1}^t\{\phi(\lambda_sY_s)-\lambda_s^2v_s^2/2\},
 \qquad E_{\tau,t}=\exp(L_{\tau,t}).\label{eq:eprocess}
\end{equation}
The implementation stores $L$ and compares it with $\log(1/\alpha)$.

\begin{theorem}[T1: conditional episode validity]
Let $\tau$ be an almost surely finite stopping time and let the comparison rule
be chosen using $\F_\tau$. Under \eqref{eq:null} and the stated conditional
second-moment bound, $E_{\tau,t}$ is a nonnegative supermartingale starting at
one. For $0<\alpha<1$,
\begin{equation}
 \Prb\!\left(\sup_{t\ge\tau}E_{\tau,t}\ge1/\alpha\mid\F_\tau\right)\le\alpha.
\end{equation}
The bound also holds when decisions are restricted to annual revisions.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $Q_t=\exp\{\phi(\lambda_tY_t)-\lambda_t^2v_t^2/2\}$,
\begin{align*}
 \E[Q_t\mid\F_{t-1}]
 &\le e^{-\lambda_t^2v_t^2/2}
 \{1+\lambda_t\E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]+\lambda_t^2\E[Y_t^2\mid\F_{t-1}]/2\}\\
 &\le e^{-\lambda_t^2v_t^2/2}(1+\lambda_t^2v_t^2/2)\le1.
\end{align*}
Multiplying by past capital proves the supermartingale property. Stop at the
first threshold crossing or a finite horizon. Conditional expectation of
stopped capital is at most one, and crossing requires capital at least
$1/\alpha$. Conditional Markov and then monotone convergence of the crossing
events prove the result. Annual crossings form a subset of all crossings.
\end{proof}

The theorem permits past-based candidate choice, annual refitting and estimated
bets. It does not combine validity across different comparisons or restarts.
A useful anytime p-value for the same episode is
$\min\{1,1/\max_{s\le t}E_{\tau,s}\}$. Thus the relevant contrast with the
benchmark is sequentially valid inference versus repeated uncorrected
fixed-time inference, rather than e-values versus every possible p-value
procedure. The operational decision uses current annual evidence, not a stale
historical maximum.

\begin{proposition}[Episode evidence does not certify the current sign]
Even with a valid moment bound and positive episode mean, an annual rejection
can occur when the current conditional gain is negative.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Consider a deterministic 252-date episode, with $Y_t=1/2$ for the first 200
dates and $Y_t=-1/2$ for the remaining 52. The valid second-moment bound
$\nu^2=1$ and a predeclared first-block bet $\lambda=.1$ give
$L_{252}=148\phi(.05)-252(.1)^2/2>6>\log20$.
The episode mean is $74/252>0$, but
$\E[Y_{252}\mid\F_{251}]=-1/2$. The episode null is false, so T1 is not
violated; the interpretation as a certificate of current benefit fails.
\end{proof}

The issue is more fundamental for prospective certification without any
restriction relating the past to the next period.
\begin{proposition}[A limit under unrestricted nonstationarity]
Fix a decision date $t$ and a class of laws closed under replacing the next
score by zero while preserving the law of $\F_t$. Let a decision
$D_t\in\{0,1\}$ be $\F_t$-measurable. If
$\Prb_Q(D_t=1)\le\alpha$ for every law $Q$ in the class satisfying
$\E_Q[Y_{t+1}\mid\F_t]\le0$, then
$\Prb_P(D_t=1)\le\alpha$ for every law $P$ in the class, including
alternatives with positive next-period gain.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any $P$, construct $Q$ with the same distribution on $\F_t$ and
$Y_{t+1}=0$. This replacement respects any positive upper second-moment bound.
The prospective null holds under $Q$. Since $D_t$ depends only on the shared
history, $\Prb_P(D_t=1)=\Prb_Q(D_t=1)\le\alpha$.
\end{proof}
The closure condition deliberately permits unrestricted changes; it need not
hold in the specified persistent DGP. The proposition identifies why a
uniformly informative certificate of next-period benefit requires temporal
structure or a different inferential target. It is not a failure of the
episode-wide guarantee in T1, and no novelty claim is attached to this basic
observational-equivalence argument.

\section{Power and predictable betting}
Work in local episode time with blocks of $h$ observations and constant bound
$\nu^2$. For the fixed-bet result, assume
\begin{equation}
 \E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]\ge\delta>0,\qquad
 \E[Y_t^2\mid\F_{t-1}]\le\nu^2.\label{eq:alt}
\end{equation}
Write $g(\lambda)=\lambda\delta-\lambda^2\nu^2$ and
$\pi_K=\Prb(\max_{k\le K}E_{hk}\ge1/\alpha)$.

\begin{theorem}[T2a: a fixed-bet power certificate]
For any constant $\lambda\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \pi_K(\lambda)\ge\pos{1-\alpha^{-1}e^{-hK g(\lambda)}}.
\end{equation}
The exponent is maximized at
$\lambda^*=\delta/(2\nu^2)$ with $g^*=\delta^2/(4\nu^2)$.
A sufficient horizon for power at least $1-\beta$ is
\begin{equation}
 hK\ge \frac{4\nu^2}{\delta^2}\log\frac1{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:phi} and \eqref{eq:alt},
\begin{align*}
 \E[e^{-\phi(\lambda Y_t)+\lambda^2\nu^2/2}\mid\F_{t-1}]
 &\le e^{\lambda^2\nu^2/2}(1-\lambda\delta+\lambda^2\nu^2/2)\\
 &\le e^{-\lambda\delta+\lambda^2\nu^2}=e^{-g(\lambda)}.
\end{align*}
Iteration gives $\E E_{hK}^{-1}\le e^{-hKg(\lambda)}$. No annual crossing
implies $E_{hK}<1/\alpha$; Markov bounds this probability by
$\alpha^{-1}e^{-hKg(\lambda)}$. Maximizing the concave quadratic and solving
the inequality for $hK$ finish the proof.
\end{proof}

This maximizes the stated lower certificate, not exact power over all tests.
For $g(\lambda)>0$, the same argument bounds the first annual crossing index
$T_h$ by $\Prb(T_h>k)\le\min\{1,\alpha^{-1}e^{-hkg(\lambda)}\}$.
Consequently, with $k_0=\lceil\log(1/\alpha)/(h g(\lambda))\rceil$,
$\E[hT_h]\le hk_0+h/(1-e^{-hg(\lambda)})$. This is not a post-change
detection-delay guarantee.

For adaptive bets, strengthen the mean assumption to
$\E[Y_t\mid\F_{t-1}]=\delta$ at every date. Let
\begin{equation}
 \widehat\lambda_k=\Pi_{[0,1/(2\nu)]}
 \left(\frac{\pos{\widehat\delta_{k-1}}}{2\nu^2}\right),\qquad
 \widehat\delta_{k-1}=\frac{\sum_{t\le h(k-1)}Y_t}{h(k-1)},\quad k\ge2.
\end{equation}
Set $\lambda_1=\delta_0/(2\nu^2)$ using a predeclared $0<\delta_0\le\nu$.
Define
\begin{equation}
 R_K=h\nu^2\sum_{k=1}^K(\widehat\lambda_k-\lambda^*)^2,\qquad
 C_K=h\nu^2(\lambda_1-\lambda^*)^2+\frac14\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\frac1k.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[T2b--c: estimation cost and adaptive power]
Under the constant-conditional-mean assumption,
\begin{equation}
 \E R_K\le C_K,\qquad
 \E\log E_{hK}\ge A_K-C_K,\qquad A_K=\frac{hK\delta^2}{4\nu^2}.
\end{equation}
For every $r>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \pi_K(\widehat\lambda)\ge
 \pos{1-C_K/r-\alpha^{-1}e^{-A_K+r}}.\label{eq:adaptivepower}
\end{equation}
Taking $r=A_K/2$ yields consistency for fixed $\delta>0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$Y_t-\delta$ are orthogonal martingale differences, so
$\E(\widehat\delta_{k-1}-\delta)^2\le\nu^2/[h(k-1)]$.
Jensen gives $\delta\le\nu$; positive-part truncation and projection onto an
interval containing the oracle do not increase squared error. Thus
$\E(\widehat\lambda_k-\lambda^*)^2\le1/[4\nu^2h(k-1)]$.
Summing proves the regret bound. The third inequality in \eqref{eq:phi} gives
conditional expected log growth at least $\lambda_t\delta-\lambda_t^2\nu^2$.
Complete the square:
\begin{equation}
 \lambda\delta-\lambda^2\nu^2
 =\frac{\delta^2}{4\nu^2}-\nu^2(\lambda-\lambda^*)^2.\label{eq:square}
\end{equation}
This proves the expected growth statement. For power, the inverse-capital
argument from T2a shows that
\[
 Z_T=E_T^{-1}\exp\!\left\{\sum_{t\le T}(\lambda_t\delta-\lambda_t^2\nu^2)\right\}
\]
is a nonnegative supermartingale with expectation at most one. At $T=hK$,
$Z_T=E_T^{-1}e^{A_K-R_K}$. Hence
\begin{align*}
 \Prb(E_{hK}<1/\alpha)
 &\le\Prb(R_K>r)+\Prb(Z_{hK}>\alpha e^{A_K-r})\\
 &\le C_K/r+\alpha^{-1}e^{-A_K+r}.
\end{align*}
No annual crossing implies the event on the left. Finally $C_K=O(\log K)$
while $A_K$ grows linearly, proving consistency.
\end{proof}

$R_K$ concerns the quadratic growth certificate. It is not asserted to equal
the realized log-capital difference from the fixed-bet oracle. In the changing
market below, neither a constant effect nor a constant conditional mean is
available. T1 still applies when its null is true, but T2b--c cannot be
transferred to regime adaptation.

\section{The economic value of an additional signal}
Fix a date and a common previous state $b$. In this section only, remove the
leverage constraint and suppose $H=\gamma M+\eta\Lambda\succ0$.
Writing $a_0=\mu+\eta\Lambda b$, the objective up to a constant is
$a_0'w-w'Hw/2-c\norm{w-b}_1$.
Let $w_S^*$ maximize it on support $S$ and let $j\notin S$ with $b_j=0$.
Define
\begin{equation}
 q_j=(a_0)_j-H_{jS}w_S^*,\qquad
 d_j=H_{jj}-H_{jS}H_{SS}^{-1}H_{Sj}>0.
\end{equation}
For empty $S$, use $q_j=(a_0)_j$ and $d_j=H_{jj}$.

\begin{theorem}[T3: entry threshold and marginal-value bounds]
The gain $\Delta_j=V(S\cup\{j\};b)-V(S;b)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\pos{|q_j|-c}^2}{2H_{jj}}
 \le\Delta_j\le\frac{\pos{|q_j|-c}^2}{2d_j}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\Delta_j>0$ if and only if $|q_j|>c$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Optimality on $S$ gives $z_S\in\partial\norm{w_S^*-b_S}_1$ with
$(a_0)_S-H_{SS}w_S^*=cz_S$. Write a perturbation as $(u,x)$ on $(S,j)$.
The subgradient inequality and $b_j=0$ bound its objective improvement by
\[
 q_jx-c|x|-\tfrac12\{u'H_{SS}u+2xH_{jS}u+H_{jj}x^2\}.
\]
Minimizing the quadratic in $u$ gives $u=-H_{SS}^{-1}H_{Sj}x$ and residual
curvature $d_j$. Maximizing $q_jx-c|x|-d_jx^2/2$ gives the upper bound.
Holding $u=0$ makes the actual improvement exactly
$q_jx-c|x|-H_{jj}x^2/2$ and gives the lower bound. Positive denominators
then prove the entry characterization.
\end{proof}

When $c=0$, the subgradient inequality is exact and
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_j=
 \frac{\{(a_0)_j-H_{jS}H_{SS}^{-1}(a_0)_S\}^2}
 {2\{H_{jj}-H_{jS}H_{SS}^{-1}H_{Sj}\}}.
\end{equation}
With incumbent weights frozen, the new-coordinate optimizer is
$\operatorname{sign}(q_j)\pos{|q_j|-c}/H_{jj}$.
The quantity that matters is residual marginal utility, rather than standalone
expected return. The leverage-constrained simulation solves the full convex
problem; these unconstrained formulas are not used as constrained shortcuts.

\section{From population values to estimated policies}
For a common feasible set, let $w^*(S;b)$ maximize $J$ and
$\widehat w(S;b)$ maximize $\widehat J$. Suppose on an event $\mathcal A$,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{w,b}|\widehat J(w;b)-J(w;b)|\le\varepsilon.\label{eq:uniform}
\end{equation}
This is an assumption about a specified event; it does not itself establish
any probabilistic coverage under a changing market.

\begin{theorem}[T4: implementation and marginal-value errors]
On $\mathcal A$, each implementation loss satisfies
$0\le\ell(S;b)=V(S;b)-J(\widehat w(S;b);b)\le2\varepsilon$.
For two supports at a common state,
\begin{equation}
 \left|J(\widehat w(B;b);b)-J(\widehat w(S;b);b)
       -\{V(B;b)-V(S;b)\}\right|\le2\varepsilon.
\end{equation}
The same bound holds with separate states $b_B,b_S$ on the corresponding two
values, if \eqref{eq:uniform} holds uniformly over those states.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Insert the estimated objective at the population and estimated maximizers:
\begin{align*}
 J(w^*;b)-J(\widehat w;b)
 &=J(w^*;b)-\widehat J(w^*;b)
   +\widehat J(w^*;b)-\widehat J(\widehat w;b)\\
 &\quad+\widehat J(\widehat w;b)-J(\widehat w;b)\le2\varepsilon.
\end{align*}
The middle difference is nonpositive. Population optimality gives the lower
bound zero. The marginal error is $\ell(S;b)-\ell(B;b)$, the difference of
two numbers in $[0,2\varepsilon]$. Replacing their states changes none of
these steps.
\end{proof}

With support-specific uniform error $\varepsilon_S$ and estimated solver
suboptimality $\xi_S$, loss is at most $2\varepsilon_S+\xi_S$.
The marginal-error bound becomes the maximum of the two loss bounds.
For known costs and $\norm w_2\le L$,
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon\le L\norm{\widehat\mu-\mu}_2
       +\frac{\gamma L^2}{2}\norm{\widehat M-M}_{\op}.
\end{equation}
If $\Lambda$ is estimated, add
$\eta(L+L_b)^2\norm{\widehat\Lambda-\Lambda}_{\op}/2$.

The same uniform event separates estimation error from the cost of restricting
the portfolio. At the fund's actual state $b$, let
$\widehat G=\widehat V([p];b)-\widehat V(S;b)\ge0$ and
$R=V([p];b)-J(\widehat w(S;b);b)$. Then
\begin{equation}
 |R-\widehat G|\le2\varepsilon.
\end{equation}
Indeed, $|V([p];b)-\widehat V([p];b)|\le\varepsilon$ and
$|J(\widehat w(S;b);b)-\widehat V(S;b)|\le\varepsilon$.
For waiting in cash, $S=\varnothing$ and $b=0$, the latter difference is exactly
zero, so the bound improves to $\varepsilon$. This does not make
$\widehat G$ an observable confidence bound: the uniform event still needs
justification. It shows how support restriction and waiting can dominate even
when estimation error is small. The comparator uses the fund's same current
state; comparing two entire traded paths requires accounting for their
different states as well.

For the link to a common state, assume $\norm w_2,\norm b_2,\norm{b'}_2\le L$
and $\norm\Lambda_{\op}\le\bar\Lambda$. Then
\begin{equation}
 |C(w,b)-C(w,b')|\le L_C\norm{b-b'}_2,\qquad
 L_C=c\sqrt p+2\eta\bar\Lambda L.
\end{equation}
The maximum over $w$ preserves this Lipschitz bound. Consequently the shadow
policy difference $d^{state}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 |d^{state}-\{V(B;b_S)-V(S;b_S)\}|
 \le2\varepsilon+L_C\norm{b_B-b_S}_2.\label{eq:bridge}
\end{equation}
This state term cannot be dropped by invoking support monotonicity.

If $\gamma M+\eta\Lambda\succeq mI$, known identical costs and exact
optimization yield a refinement. Let
$e=\norm{\widehat\mu-\mu}_2+\gamma L\norm{\widehat M-M}_{\op}$.
The estimated first-order variational inequality implies that, along every
feasible direction, the true negative objective's subgradient is bounded
below by the negative gradient error. Strong convexity gives
$\ell\le e\norm{w^*-\widehat w}_2-m\norm{w^*-\widehat w}_2^2/2
\le e^2/(2m)$.
In fixed-dimensional independent data with finite fourth moments and no
persistent moment bias, expected squared moment errors are $O(n^{-1})$.
Neither that rate nor consistency follows from expanding estimation under
unrestricted drift.

\section{An auditable annual selection rule}
All methods use initial training $n_0=h=252$, expanding sample moments and
annual parameter updates. The estimated second moment is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat M_\rho=(1-\rho)n^{-1}\sum_{s\le n}A_sA_s'
       +\rho\operatorname{diag}\!\left(n^{-1}\sum_{s\le n}A_sA_s'\right)
       +\epsilon I.
\end{equation}
Shrinkage and ridge are common to methods and frozen before evaluation.
Starting from $S=\varnothing$, each block opens all additions, all removals,
and swaps using the ten excluded signals with the largest training marginal
values. The shortlist is known before the validating returns. Retained
comparisons continue their own evidence; a swap leaving the shortlist closes
its episode and starts afresh if later reintroduced.

At a block end, choose the eligible addition with largest positive episode
mean score. If none exists, consider removals, then swaps. Ties use stable
signal identifiers. Eligibility for PARK/REVIVE requires current
$L\ge\log20$. At most one support change occurs per annual revision.
The next block charges its actual transition from the fund's previous target.
In a 15-year market with one training year, only 14 structural changes are
possible, including any terminal decision without a subsequent realized
investment period. Recovering all 45 predictive functions is not possible
under this calendar.

For $s=|S|$, a pass considers $p+s\min(10,p-s)$ candidate policies plus the
shared reference. This is a count of candidate evaluations, not total runtime.
Swap10 is a search heuristic without a global-optimality guarantee.

\section{One simulated financial market}
For $N=500$ stocks and $D=30$ observed characteristics, excess returns are
\begin{equation}
 R^e_{i,t+1}=\beta_i'F_{t+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{100}\theta_{j,t}f_j(Z_{i,t})
       +\epsilon_{i,t+1}.
\end{equation}
Twenty latent characteristics drive candidate predictors and ten form an
independent noise block. Each block follows a stationary Gaussian AR(1) with
persistence $0.97$, unit marginal variance and correlation $0.6$ within
five-coordinate groups. Observed characteristics are its componentwise
hyperbolic tangent. A design-seeded library contains normalized linear
functions, saturating functions and interactions, all bounded by one.

There are 15 persistent, 10 weak, 10 decay and 10 revive functions, together
with 40 independent structural-noise functions and 15 correlated proxies.
Noise functions use only the independent characteristic block. Each proxy
combines $0.9$ times a predictor with $0.1$ times an independent noise
function. All 55 structural nulls have exactly zero coefficient and zero
coefficient innovation, while their characteristics continue to vary.

A single symmetric two-state Markov chain starts in state zero and switches
with daily probability $1/1008$. Stress raises volatility. A first-stress
indicator switches decay targets permanently to zero; revive targets are
high in normal states and low in stress. Predictive coefficients follow a
projected AR(1) with half-life 126 days, bounded symmetric innovations and
caps fixed in configuration. No seed is selected for having a particular
regime history.

The four factor shocks and stock-specific shocks are independent standardized
Laplace variables. Normal-state factor standard deviations are
$(.009,.004,.004,.004)$ and stock-specific standard deviation is $.012$;
stress multiplies them by $1.5$. Factor means are zero and fixed stock betas
are bounded by $(1.5,1,1,1)$ in absolute value. For function values $z_{ij,t}$,
the common sleeve weights are
\begin{equation}
 P_{ij,t}=\frac{z_{ij,t}-N^{-1}\sum_lz_{lj,t}}
 {\max\{\sum_l|z_{lj,t}-N^{-1}\sum_kz_{kj,t}|,10^{-12}\}},
 \qquad A_{t+1}=P_t'R^e_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
Each sleeve is dollar neutral and has gross leverage at most one. Sleeves are
not fully factor neutralized. All investible algorithms receive realized sleeve
returns and public risk caps; the extension additionally uses current public
function values and sleeve exposures. Realized coefficients, regimes and exact
conditional moments are confined to the evaluator and oracle.

\subsection{A public conditional moment certificate}
Suppose both portfolios have conditional return second and fourth moments
bounded by $m_2,m_4$ and costs bounded by $C_{max}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \E[Y_t^2\mid\F_{t-1}]\le
 12(m_2+\gamma^2m_4/4+C_{max}^2)=\nu^2,\qquad
 C_{max}=2cL+2\eta\bar\Lambda L^2.\label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
This follows from $U^2\le3(r^2+\gamma^2r^4/4+C^2)$ and
$(U_B-U_S)^2\le2U_B^2+2U_S^2$.

Let $m$ bound absolute conditional portfolio mean and $v$ bound variance using
the declared coefficient caps, stock beta caps, maximum volatility and gross
leverage. Independent standardized Laplace shocks have fourth cumulant three,
so one may take
$m_2=m^2+v$ and $m_4=m^4+6m^2v+6v^2$.
These public quantities do not reveal a realized hidden coefficient or regime.
The resulting constant is deliberately conservative. A sample variance is
not substituted for it. The ratio of the certificate to true score second
moments is reported to diagnose power.

\subsection{Benchmarks and evaluation}
All Signals optimizes over the entire library. Greedy uses the same search and
accepts a positive mean score without a test threshold. Repeated-HAC-p uses
the same policy comparisons and repeated annual one-sided normal p-values,
with Bartlett long-run variance and bandwidth
$\lfloor4(n/100)^{2/9}\rfloor$. Its repeated testing is uncorrected and
finite-sample nominal validity is not claimed. The E-process method uses
\eqref{eq:eprocess}. Oracle uses true current moments with identical costs and
constraints, without future shocks. It maximizes one-period conditional
utility, not pathwise wealth. A separate oracle matched to each method's
actual state supplies implementation regret.

The primary outcome is mean OOS utility. Secondary outcomes are net wealth,
conventional and HAC-adjusted Sharpe, drawdown, costs, turnover, inactivity and
selected support size. A variance-zero Sharpe is undefined. Net wealth is
absorbed at zero on insolvency; failed paths are retained. Structural discovery
and conditional economic quality are reported separately. Two consecutive
favorable annual economic comparisons define exploratory evaluator events.
Those legacy event logs condition on survival of an unchanged comparison;
rapid accepted moves can close the comparison before a second block exists.
They therefore do not identify comparable detection-delay distributions and
are not used as evidence of detection performance. A future delay study must
continue the relevant counterfactual policies after their comparisons close.
These path-based labels also do not define an ex ante type-I-error calibration
experiment.

All methods share each simulated market. Uncertainty about performance uses
independent paired seeds, not individual daily observations. Smoke runs test
software with reduced dimensions. Pilot and final evaluation use disjoint
seed pools with full dimensions; the final design calls for 500 new seeds.
Execution status is reported explicitly below.

\section{Discussion}
The central economic distinction is between a direction that can help an
informed optimizer and a policy that helps when estimated and traded. T3
characterizes the former; T4 and the separate state accounts connect it to the
latter. Sequential evidence addresses a further question: whether observed
utility differences justify rejecting an episode-wide conditional null.
These questions remain distinct even when their calculations share data.

Several obstacles remain before a strong publication claim is warranted.
The baseline moment certificate prevents entry in the executed pilot; the
declared higher-moment extension makes first certification feasible in new
markets but still requires substantial waiting. The separate full-strategy
study measures post-entry performance and opportunity costs; a small executed
pilot does not establish practical superiority. The annual
calendar restricts the number of experiments that can become positions, and
the episode null does not directly express a current-regime profitability
claim. No multiplicity guarantee protects the whole selection process. A
complete final simulation and a broader prior-art review are necessary before
claiming either practical superiority or methodological novelty. Empirical
applications are outside this paper's current scope.

The separate horizon-betting experiment improves rejection probability under
its declared stationary design law while retaining individual validity.
Its working score model and one-dimensional evidence state do not describe
the evolving financial policy pairs. Its power gain therefore does not
establish better financial decisions or remove the cost of a conservative
public risk envelope, a positive minimum-effect floor or changing regimes.
The costless Gaussian example isolates a different limit: estimation can
leave only logarithmically growing information. Its sharp confidence exponent
does not ensure useful finite-horizon power, as its controlled experiment
demonstrates. Its conditional-benefit coefficient is not an expected stopped
cost or a theorem for the stateful financial fund.

\section{Conditional moments used only by the evaluator}
Let $x=\mu_x+h_x'\zeta$ and $y=\mu_y+h_y'\zeta$, where the components of
$\zeta$ are independent, symmetric, variance one and fourth moment six.
Write $v_x=h_x'h_x$, $v_y=h_y'h_y$ and $v_{xy}=h_x'h_y$. Then
\begin{align*}
 \E x^2&=\mu_x^2+v_x,&\E x^3&=\mu_x^3+3\mu_xv_x,\\
 \E x^4&=\mu_x^4+6\mu_x^2v_x+3v_x^2+3\sum_lh_{xl}^4,\\
 \E[x^2y]&=\mu_x^2\mu_y+\mu_yv_x+2\mu_xv_{xy},\\
 \E[xy^2]&=\mu_x\mu_y^2+\mu_xv_y+2\mu_yv_{xy},\\
 \E[x^2y^2]&=\mu_x^2\mu_y^2+\mu_x^2v_y+\mu_y^2v_x+4\mu_x\mu_yv_{xy}\\
 &\quad+v_xv_y+2v_{xy}^2+3\sum_lh_{xl}^2h_{yl}^2.
\end{align*}
These follow by expanding products and keeping only even multiplicities of
each independent centered shock. With $D=C_B-C_S$, $L=x-y$, $Q=x^2-y^2$,
\begin{align*}
 \E Y&=\E L-\gamma\E Q/2-D,\\
 \E Y^2&=\E L^2+\gamma^2\E Q^2/4+D^2
             -\gamma\E[LQ]-2D\E L+\gamma D\E Q,\\
 \E L^2&=(\mu_x-\mu_y)^2+v_x+v_y-2v_{xy},\\
 \E Q^2&=\E x^4+\E y^4-2\E[x^2y^2],\\
 \E[LQ]&=\E x^3+\E y^3-\E[xy^2]-\E[x^2y].
\end{align*}
The implementation verifies these expressions against an independent
conditional Monte Carlo sample. Identical policies and states give exactly
$Y=0$, a separate cancellation check.

\section{Reproducibility and numerical conventions}
Versioned configurations, source hashes, installed versions and seed manifests
identify each run. Unfinished runs resume at atomic annual checkpoints without
counting a score twice; completed weight, score and event ledgers are retained.
The learner is deterministic given its observed history. The market,
statistical diagnostics and independent moment checks use distinct random
streams. Original input provenance is preserved in the accompanying repository.

The optimizer solves a convex quadratic program with epigraph variables for
turnover and gross leverage. It records primal and dual residuals and the
duality gap. Exact zero-trade cases have a sufficient subgradient check.
Weights, realized score histories and decisions are tested for invariance to
future-data perturbations. Stable identifiers govern tie breaking, and the
candidate shortlist is tested under a consistent permutation of columns.
No old empirical or simulated result enters this manuscript.
