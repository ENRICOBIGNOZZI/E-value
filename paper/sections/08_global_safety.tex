\section{What a search-wide guarantee would cost}
\label{sec:global-budget}
The experiments above use an individual level of $0.05$. A global budget is
possible, but it changes the decision rule and its waiting cost. The following
is a standard spending argument, included to make that distinction explicit.

\begin{proposition}[Episode spending]
Enumerate adaptively opened episodes by $k\ge1$, assign deterministic budgets
$a_k>0$ with $\sum_k a_k\le\alpha$, and reject episode $k$ only when its
capital reaches $1/a_k$. Fix a data-generating law $P$ and a set $I_0$ of episode
indices whose conditional null and moment assumptions hold $P$-almost surely
throughout their active dates. An unopened episode is never rejected. Then
\[
 P\{\text{some episode in }I_0\text{ is rejected}\}\le\alpha.
\]
The claim holds for each valid capital construction under its own assumptions,
including adaptive opening, stopping and closing of comparisons.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Conditional Ville's inequality at the opening time bounds the rejection
probability of each valid null episode by $a_k$. Stopping its capital at
closure preserves this bound. Taking expectations and a countable union bound
gives $\sum_{k\in I_0}a_k\le\alpha$. Restricting rejections further by the
positive-history filter or annual priority rule can only reduce this event.
No independence between episodes is required.
\end{proof}

This controls rejection of valid episode nulls, not selection of a policy with
negative gain on the current date. The latter remains outside T1, as the
earlier sign-change counterexample shows. In particular, an ex post negative
annual gain is not by itself a label for membership in $I_0$.

For illustration, reserve half the total budget for the initial 100 episodes,
so each receives $a_k=\alpha/200$, and allocate the other half summably to all
later episodes. The first-entry threshold becomes $200/\alpha=4000$, or
$\log E\ge8.294$, rather than $\log E\ge2.996$. This illustration was examined
after the mechanism study; it is not a predeclared simulated comparator.
The largest annual log capital in that complete fixed-reference study is
4.918. Consequently none of its 20 markets would produce a first entry under
this particular spending rule. Until the first entry, the fixed-empty-reference
policies and scores are unchanged, so this is an exact replay implication and
does not require a counterfactual post-entry fund path. Other allocations can
give different results; this example is not an optimal-budget theorem.

In the stationary power certificates, replacing $\alpha$ by $a_k$ increases
the required log evidence by $\log(\alpha/a_k)$. The rare-loss necessary
horizon similarly replaces $\log\{(1-\beta)/\alpha\}$ by
$\log\{(1-\beta)/a_k\}$. These identities explain a further information cost
of protecting a search. They do not quantify actual delay in the changing
financial DGP or establish that the uncorrected search has global error
exactly equal to its nominal individual level.
